The dashboard prepares the active data, applies the selected filters, computes the relevant outputs, and renders the selected plot, table, or map. After a run is complete, you may change Display to review another available output without changing the input data.
01

Temporal Outbreak Detection — CUSUM Display

Detect when case counts exceed the expected baseline

Concept

What is CUSUM?

CUSUM stands for Cumulative Sum. Think of it as a running tally that measures how far current case counts are drifting from what you'd normally expect.

Imagine you're watching a water tank: CUSUM measures how much water has accumulated above the normal level. A small, steady drip goes unnoticed. But when water starts piling up week after week, CUSUM raises an alarm — even before the tank overflows.

In plain words: CUSUM asks "is this week's count too high, considering everything that came before?" It catches subtle, gradual increases that a simple threshold would miss.

The three scenarios we will compare

Scenario A — No signal
Comuna 10
RR = 1.0 all year. Cases fluctuate randomly around the expected baseline. CUSUM should stay flat and below the alert threshold.
Scenario B — Moderate signal
Comuna 14
RR = 1.5 starting Week 20. A 50% increase in cases. CUSUM should accumulate and cross the threshold after a few weeks.
Scenario C — Strong signal
Comuna 12
RR = 3.0 starting Week 12. Cases triple. CUSUM should cross the threshold quickly and stay elevated.

Scenarios

Comparing the Three Communes

Scenario A — Comuna 10 (No signal, RR = 1.0)

With no injected outbreak, cases fluctuate randomly around 100 per 100,000. The CUSUM statistic should wander close to zero and never cross the alert threshold throughout all 52 weeks. Any brief excursion above the threshold would be a false alarm — expected to happen occasionally by chance.

What to look for: A flat chart with the CUSUM line staying well below the red alert line. Observed prevalence hovering around ~100/100k with no trend.

Scenario B — Comuna 14 (Moderate signal, RR = 1.5 from Week 20)

Cases increase by 50% starting at Week 20 (observed prevalence rises from ~100 to ~150 per 100,000). The CUSUM statistic should begin accumulating steadily from Week 20 onward and cross the threshold within a few weeks — typically around weeks 23–26, depending on random variation.

What to look for: The CUSUM line drifts upward after Week 20 and eventually crosses the alert threshold. The signal is real but takes a few weeks to confirm — this is CUSUM's strength: it accumulates evidence before alarming.

Scenario C — Comuna 12 (Strong signal, RR = 3.0 from Week 12)

Cases triple starting at Week 12 (observed prevalence rises from ~100 to ~300 per 100,000). The CUSUM statistic should cross the alert threshold rapidly, likely within 1–2 weeks of onset, and remain elevated for the rest of the year.

What to look for: A sharp, sustained rise in the CUSUM chart starting at Week 12. The alert is triggered quickly and stays active — a clear epidemic signal.

Understanding the CUSUM formula

The CUSUM statistic at week t is calculated as:

FormulaTabular CUSUM (one-sided, upper)
S(t) = max(0,  S(t-1) + [x(t) - μ₀ - k])

where:
  x(t)  = observed prevalence at week t
  μ₀    = expected prevalence (baseline, e.g. 100 per 100k)
  k     = allowance parameter (e.g. 0.5 × μ₀ = 50)
          → allows for random variation before accumulating
  S(t)  = CUSUM statistic (resets to 0 when it would go negative)

Alert is triggered when:  S(t) ≥ h  (e.g. h = 4 × μ₀ = 400)
In plain words: Each week, the algorithm subtracts the "allowance" (k) from the excess above baseline. Only consistent, sustained excess accumulates into the score S(t). Random noise stays near zero; a real outbreak keeps pushing S(t) upward until it crosses h.
02

Spatial Cluster Detection — Geospatial Display

Identify which locations are part of a geographic outbreak cluster

Concept

What is LISA / Moran's I?

CUSUM tells you when something is happening. LISA tells you where — and more importantly, whether high-prevalence areas are surrounded by other high-prevalence areas (a true spatial cluster), or just isolated spikes.

Moran's I measures spatial autocorrelation: do neighboring areas tend to have similar values? A value near +1 means "yes, similar areas cluster together." A value near −1 means "high areas are surrounded by low areas."

LISA (Local Indicators of Spatial Association) is the local version: it scores every individual commune and classifies it into one of four cluster types.

LISA CategoryColorMeaningInterpretation
High-High■ RedHot spotHigh prevalence, surrounded by high-prevalence neighbors. Core of an outbreak cluster.
Low-Low■ BlueCold spotLow prevalence area surrounded by low-prevalence neighbors. Unaffected cluster.
High-Low■ OrangeSpatial outlierHigh prevalence but surrounded by low neighbors. Isolated spike, not a cluster.
Low-High■ Light BlueSpatial outlierLow prevalence surrounded by high neighbors. May be under-reported or buffered.
Not Significant■ GreyNo patternNo statistically significant spatial association (p > 0.05).
Expected result for our dataset: Communes 12, 13, 14, and 15 (contiguous, all with elevated RR) should appear as High-High hot spots in later weeks. Communes 10, 11, 2, 5, 6, 7 (all RR = 1.0) should appear as Not Significant or Low-Low.

Interpretation

Interpretation Guide

What should you see across the three runs?

// Run A — Week 10 (pre-outbreak)

At Week 10, only Comuna 12's outbreak has not yet started (onset is Week 12). All communes should show prevalence close to 100 per 100,000. The Global Moran's I should be not significant (grey banner). The LISA map should be mostly grey (Not Significant) — no spatial structure yet.

// Run B — Week 25 (all outbreaks active)

By Week 25, all three outbreaks are running (commune 12 since week 12, commune 13 since week 18, communes 14–15 since week 20). You should observe:

Global Moran's I: Statistically significant (green banner, p < 0.05) — confirming that high-prevalence communes cluster together spatially.
LISA Map: Communes 12, 13, 14, and 15 should appear in red (High-High). Communes 2, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11 should appear grey (Not Significant) or blue (Low-Low).

// Run C — Weeks 20–52 (accumulated signal)

Aggregating over a longer period amplifies the signal. The cluster should be even more clearly defined, with stronger LISA statistics and lower p-values for the outbreak communes. This demonstrates how the tool supports cumulative surveillance over a surveillance period, not just single-week snapshots.

Key takeaways

✓
CUSUM detects the "when" — Geospatial detects the "where"
Use both tools together: CUSUM alerts you that something is happening in a commune; Geospatial confirms it is part of a broader spatial cluster and helps identify neighboring communes at risk.
✓
High-High does not mean "certainly an outbreak"
A significant p-value means the spatial pattern is unlikely to occur by chance. But you must combine it with contextual knowledge (population density, reporting quality, case definitions) before drawing conclusions.
✓
The time window matters
A single-week snapshot can be noisy. Aggregating over a range of weeks smooths out random variation and reveals sustained patterns. Choose the window based on your epidemiological question.
✓
Population size affects detection power
Small communes (few census fractions, small population) produce noisier prevalence estimates. A RR = 1.5 signal in a small commune is harder to detect than in a large one.
